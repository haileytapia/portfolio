# Microsoft Learn contributor guidance
{: .no_toc }

[Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/) hosts documentation for hundreds of products across Azure, Visual Studio, Windows, Linux, and more. Over time, its extensive body of contributor guidance had also evolved, creating an opportunity to rethink how that guidance should work across a large and dynamic documentation ecosystem.

## Three workstreams

I was part of a five-person working group recruited by leadership as part of a broader initiative to define the foundation for the next generation of contributor guidance. Writers with different areas of expertise were assigned to workstreams aligned with their strengths, with the goal of developing guidance that could be implemented across Microsoft Learn in the future.

Our group focused on three interrelated aspects of how documentation is created and organized.

### Content types

Over time, our internal library of content types (quickstarts, tutorials, how-to guides, etc.) had become unwieldy. Some types overlapped, and some were no longer maintained.

We reviewed the existing content types and established clearer criteria for their purpose, metadata, templates, and lifecycle. We also clarified distinctions between different forms of documentation, including generated reference content, "What's New" updates, and release notes. This gave contributors a clearer basis for deciding what type of content to create for a customer need and how it should be structured.

### Information flow

Microsoft Learn documentation is often assembled from contributions by PMs, engineers, and writers, with each contributor focused on a particular feature, task, or area of expertise. This can make individual articles useful on their own while leaving the larger documentation experience fragmented. Customers might encounter related content without a clear sense of what to read next or how the pieces fit together.

This workstream focused on how content should connect across the customer journey, from discovering a product or capability through implementation, use, and ongoing changes. We developed principles for establishing canonical paths, linking related content, and connecting articles that support the same customer scenario. The goal was to help contributors think beyond the article in front of them and consider how each piece fits into the broader narrative of the product documentation.

### Information architecture

Our group brought different perspectives on what constitutes information architecture and how it should be defined. We conducted a survey to find common ground, and a shared principle emerged: information architecture should start with customer tasks. We translated this principle into guidance for navigation, TOC structure, landing pages, hub pages, and taxonomies, with the goal of organizing information around the way customers approach a task.

We also considered the needs of AI agents alongside those of human readers. We didn't want AI to require a separate information architecture, so wherever possible, we considered how the same structure could serve both. This included thinking about how content should be organized and connected so that people can navigate it while agents can understand how information relates to a customer's task.

## Impact

Our work established a foundation for modernizing contributor guidance across Microsoft Learn. Rather than simply adding to an already extensive body of standards, we reexamined how guidance should approach content types, information flow, and information architecture as documentation and customer needs evolved.

For each workstream, we documented the resulting principles, edge cases, implementation options, tooling needs, and recommendations for future guidance. Together, these materials gave leadership a concrete foundation for determining how the recommendations could be developed and implemented across Microsoft Learn.
